START WITH ACCESSIBILITY

ADA website compliance begins with whether people can actually use the website.

The Americans with Disabilities Act prohibits disability discrimination. For businesses open to the public, Title III requires equal access to the goods and services they offer.

DOJ’s longstanding position extends those obligations to online goods and services. Technical work focuses on removing barriers in how people access and use the website.

Official reference: DOJ guidance on web accessibility and the ADA.

Screen readers

Someone who is blind

Can important content and controls be understood and operated with a screen reader?

Keyboard access

Someone who cannot use a mouse

Can navigation, forms, menus, products, and checkout be completed with a keyboard?

Zoom & contrast

Someone with low vision

Can content be enlarged, reflowed, and understood with sufficient contrast?

Captions & media

Someone who is deaf or hard of hearing

Is important prerecorded media appropriately captioned where required?

Clear interactions

Someone with cognitive or learning disabilities

Are instructions, errors, interactions, and content understandable and predictable?

The goal is not simply a better scanner score.
The goal is a website people can use.

ADA ≠ WCAG

The ADA is the law.
WCAG is the technical accessibility standard.

These terms are often blended together, but they are not the same thing.

DOJ’s Title III guidance does not prescribe a detailed website technical standard for private businesses. It points to standards such as WCAG as guidance. Title II has a separate web and mobile rule for state and local governments.

WCAG—the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines—is W3C’s international technical standard for web accessibility. W3C encourages the latest version when an organization can choose its target.

Official reference: DOJ web accessibility guidance.

THE TECHNICAL TARGET

Use a clear accessibility standard
instead of guessing.

WCAG provides testable accessibility success criteria at Levels A, AA, and AAA. When a private organization is free to choose, WCAG 2.2 Level AA is a strong current technical target.

It builds on earlier versions and adds newer criteria. It is not a universal statutory requirement for every private-business website under ADA Title III.

  1. EARLIER WCAG 2 BASELINE2.0

    The foundation of the WCAG 2 series.

  2. BUILDS ON WCAG 2.02.1

    Adds criteria addressing mobile, low vision, and other accessibility needs.

  3. LATEST W3C RECOMMENDATION2.2

    Adds criteria for modern interactions, focus, input, and authentication.

Technical targetWCAG 2.2 AA, when appropriate

Applicable requirementConfirm the law, jurisdiction, agreement, or other requirement

Older WCAG versions remain existing standards. WCAG 2.2 removed 4.1.1 Parsing; engagements required to report against 2.0 or 2.1 may still need to address it. Read the W3C WCAG overview and WCAG 2.2 compatibility notes.

WHAT THE WORK ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

We don’t stop at identifying the barrier.

Explore four illustrative examples, from the original barrier to the corrected component and verification.

Compare the component

A menu that works without a mouse.

Test
Open and operate the main navigation using only the keyboard, then check its name and expanded state with a screen reader.
Finding
A hover-only submenu cannot be opened from the keyboard.
User impact
Some visitors cannot reach the pages inside the menu.
Remediation
Use a native button, communicate the expanded state, and support keyboard opening, closing, and predictable focus.
Verify
Retest opening the menu, following its links, closing with Escape, and returning focus to the trigger.

An error a visitor can understand and correct.

Test
Submit a required field empty and inspect the visible label, error association, and focus behavior.
Finding
The field has no persistent label and the error is not associated with the input.
User impact
A visitor may not know what information is needed or why the form cannot continue.
Remediation
Provide a visible label, a clear error, a programmatic association, and appropriate invalid-state handling.
Verify
Retest invalid and valid entries, error correction, focus, and screen-reader output.

Product options every customer can select.

Test
Use the product page with keyboard navigation and a screen reader.
Finding
Color swatches are clickable with a mouse but cannot be selected with the keyboard and do not expose their selected state.
User impact
Some customers may be unable to configure the product before adding it to the cart.
Remediation
Correct the component with appropriate interactive controls, accessible names, keyboard behavior, and programmatic selected states.
Verify
Retest product selection, Add to Cart, and related announcements with the keyboard and relevant assistive technology.

A dialog with a clear way in and out.

Test
Open the dialog, follow the keyboard path, close it, and check where focus returns.
Finding
Focus moves behind the open dialog and the close control has no accessible name.
User impact
Visitors may lose their place or be unable to finish the task.
Remediation
Correct the dialog semantics, accessible name, focus entry, close behavior, and return path.
Verify
Retest keyboard entry, contained focus, closing, and focus returning to the original trigger.

ADA website accessibility.

DOJ has consistently taken the position that ADA requirements apply to goods, services, privileges, programs, and activities offered through websites. Title III applies to businesses open to the public, while Title II applies to state and local governments.

DOJ's current Title III web guidance does not set a specific WCAG version as a detailed regulatory standard for private-business websites. WCAG is nevertheless the principal technical accessibility standard used to evaluate and improve web accessibility. Legal obligations can depend on the organization, jurisdiction, agreement, court order, or other applicable requirement.

If no specific requirement dictates another version, WCAG 2.2 Level AA is a strong current technical target. W3C encourages use of its latest WCAG version, and WCAG 2.2 was designed to remain backward compatible with earlier WCAG 2 versions.

No scanner can evaluate the entire accessibility experience. DOJ warns that a clean automated report does not necessarily mean everything is accessible, and W3C describes conformance evaluation as involving both automated testing and human evaluation.
